The family tree is a bush

How many prehuman species were there, and what are the branches?

Many species lived at the same time, and most were side branches that left no descendants. Scientists have named roughly 20 to 30 hominin species (our side of the split from chimps), and the count shifts as fossils turn up and experts argue over where to draw lines.

The Homo species

Is erectus a subcategory of sapiens? How many other Homo categories are there?

No. Homo erectus and Homo sapiens are separate species inside the same genus, Homo. Think of the genus as a surname and the species as a first name; a subspecies is a label within a single species. Many scientists think erectus is an ancestor of some later Homo species, but it is its own species.

That is roughly 12 to 15 species, depending on whether you ask a “splitter” or a “lumper.”

The subspecies twist. Because Neanderthals and our ancestors had fertile children, some scientists call Neanderthals a subspecies (Homo sapiens neanderthalensis) and us Homo sapiens sapiens. Others keep them separate. “Species” is a human label on a messy continuum.

Our common ancestor with chimps

What was the name of the ancestor we share with chimpanzees?

It has no name, because no fossil has been confirmed as that ancestor. Scientists call it the chimpanzee-human last common ancestor (CHLCA, or just “LCA”). It probably lived 6 to 7 million years ago (estimates stretch to 8 or 9) in African forests: a medium-sized ape that climbed well and ate mostly fruit and plants. It was not much like a modern chimp or human, and chimps have had as much time to evolve as we have.

The gap exists partly because forest soils destroy bones, so chimp and gorilla fossils are extremely rare. “Missing link” is a misleading old term: evolution isn’t one chain with one missing piece.

Did the groups meet and interbreed?

Did they interact socially and physically? What were the offspring like, and were they accepted?

Yes, and ancient DNA has made this one of the biggest discoveries of the past 15 years.

  • Most people with non-African ancestry carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA. Some Pacific Islanders and Aboriginal Australians carry up to several percent Denisovan DNA.
  • The children were fertile, or we wouldn’t carry the genes. A fossil from Denisova Cave nicknamed “Denny” had a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.
  • Some inherited genes helped: immune variants, and a Denisovan gene that helps Tibetans cope with high altitude.
  • Others were weeded out over time, especially some on the X chromosome and some tied to male fertility, so hybrids may have been somewhat less fit.
Were hybrids accepted? We don’t know; DNA can’t record attitudes. Denny survived to adolescence, so she was at least cared for. There is no strong evidence of large-scale warfare between the groups, but nothing rules out conflict. The best picture is small, scattered groups meeting occasionally over tens of thousands of years, sometimes pairing up and sometimes not.

Why the other groups disappeared

When did each group go extinct, and why?

Extinction was usually slow and had several causes. Be skeptical of any single clear cause; much of this is still debated.

  • Paranthropus (~1 Mya): specialized in tough vegetation, so climate shifts hit hard. Competition with Homo may have played a part.
  • Neanderthals (~40,000 years ago): small, isolated, inbred populations, harsh climate swings, competition from modern humans, and absorption into the larger sapiens population. “Wiped out” is now seen as too simple.
  • Denisovans: likewise absorbed, since they live on in some of us.
  • Island humans (Flores, ~50,000 years ago): the timing overlaps with modern humans’ arrival, but the cause is unclear.

Tools and fire

What about the development of tools?

  1. ~3.3–2.6 MyaOldest known stone tools: simple flakes and choppers (Oldowan). Which species made the earliest ones is unclear.
  2. ~1.7 MyaTeardrop-shaped hand axes (Acheulean) by Homo erectus, the longest-lasting tool tradition, over a million years.
  3. ~400,000 years agoControlled fire is clear, possibly much earlier. Cooking made food easier to digest, likely helping brain growth.
  4. ~300,000 years agoWooden spears (Schöningen, Germany) and prepared-core stone technology.
  5. ~100,000–40,000 years agoOchre pigments, engraved shells, beads, bone needles, bows and arrows, and eventually cave art. Neanderthals made jewelry and glue and possibly some art, so these abilities weren’t unique to us.

Family structure

Was there a family structure?

Nothing fossilizes a family, so all of this is inference.

  • Early hominins likely lived in groups like chimps or gorillas, with some pair-bonding.
  • As brains grew, babies were born less developed and needed years of care. That probably drove cooperative child-rearing by fathers, grandmothers, and siblings, with food sharing and home-base camps.
  • Neanderthal DNA from Chagyrskaya Cave in Siberia revealed a small community including a father and teenage daughter, and the pattern suggests females moved to join their mates’ groups.
  • Typical hunter-gatherer bands, our best model, had about 25–50 people, within wider networks of a few hundred.

The spoken word

How did speech develop?

This is the biggest mystery, because speech leaves no fossils. Clues come from throat bones, ear structure, brain shape, and genes.

  • Neanderthals had a hyoid bone like ours and the same version of the language-related gene FOXP2, so they probably spoke in some form.
  • Many researchers think complex language existed by at least 100,000 years ago. Some think a simpler proto-language goes back to Homo erectus.
  • Symbolic behavior (jewelry, ochre, art) suggests language-like thinking, and gestures, sounds, and speech likely developed together.
