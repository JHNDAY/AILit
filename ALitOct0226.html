Foundations · The Meaning of Words and How AI Generates Text
Why "Dog" Is Never Just a Dog
John Day40:06–44:00
Let's begin with the meaning of words. Take "dog." Level one is the definition: a four-legged creature, and many of us own dogs, though the dogs think they own us. Level two is contextual transformation: "you sly dog" changes the meaning altogether, to someone clever and not quite on the up and up, just because of the surrounding words. Level three is the vast number of ways "dog" appears in everything people have written. In principle you could find every recent sentence containing it, but you'd never finish. On a computer it's practical, and that is what a large language model database lets us do: probe the patterns in how words are used.

The takeaway: written words contain much more information than meets the eye, because so many other people have used them. That's a subtle concept, and it often takes repeated effort to get fixed in your head.
John Day~44:00–48:04
The basic activity of AI is generation, the G in ChatGPT. I built an interface with Claude where you type a start, here "the mysterious letter arrived on a," and ask for the next word. English takes about a hundred thousand tokens, or numbers, to describe. When I click generate, AI retrieves all 100,000 candidates from its large language model database, each with the relative frequency of its appearance, and shows the top five. Each is reasonable, like "Tuesday." You won't see "golf ball" near the top, though it's in the list, way down. Pick Tuesday and you get more predictions: morning, afternoon, evening. Token by token it builds a response that is fluent, but that doesn't guarantee it's correct. That is an extraordinarily important distinction.
John C. & John Day48:05–50:19
John C. asked whether this is like Google putting the most frequently used result first. John Day: In an extended way, yes; Gemini and all the chatbots do this, drawing on a database derived from the written record that human beings supply. Without the collected wisdom of what people have studied and written, there would be no AI. It's always statistical inference, and as Mark Twain would say, lies, damn lies, and statistics. John C. wondered how to make things more personal rather than statistical; John Day said that's coming in the next part.
Token by Token
"The mysterious letter arrived on a" → Tuesday → morning · afternoon · evening → in … Each step picks from ranked candidates; "golf ball" is possible but far down the list.
"Token by token, it's building up a response that is fluent, but that doesn't guarantee that it's correct." John Day
Three Levels of a Word
  • Definition: what the word means on its own
  • Context: surrounding words transform the meaning ("you sly dog")
  • Usage at scale: the full pattern of how people have used it, which only a computer can survey
  • Generation is statistical: ranked next-token candidates, chosen one at a time
  • Fluent is not the same as correct

Foundations · What Happens Between Your Prompt and the Reply
Words In, Numbers Computed, Words Out
John Day~53:00–~56:00
Here's a prompt box. I'll type: how do AI companies decide what documents or data sources they use in creating large language models? Now I'll tell it to tokenize. Before the prompt goes to the model, the words get turned into numbers. The AI agent then does heavy computation against the large language model database and returns a set of tokens, which are translated back into words we can read. Every transaction with AI has this layer in it, and you can try the exercise yourself.

Agents also increasingly remember what you've talked about. I'm developing something with Claude, and when I say I want to investigate a new idea, it may come back with "that's like what you did last year." For research that's your collected notes made accessible. That memory of your previous conversations is stored as tokens. Tokens to words, words to tokens: that's the foundation of how AI works, and I think it's essential for everybody to get a good grasp of it.
The Round Trip
Your words → tokens (numbers) → computation against the language model → tokens → words you can read. Remembered conversations are kept as tokens too.
"Tokens to words, words to tokens. That's the foundation of how AI works." John Day
Foundations in Brief
  • Every prompt is converted to numbers before the model touches it
  • The model computes against stored patterns and returns tokens, which are turned back into words
  • Memory of earlier chats is also stored as tokens
  • Digital intelligence is computation, not the organic kind of intelligence people have
  • Learners are encouraged to rerun the tokenizer exercise on the portal

Skills · Writing Prompts and Taming Verbose Answers
Include Yourself in the Prompt
John Day~56:00–~60:00
Now prompting: I've put together a little guide. Start by writing free-flowing narrative in your preferred writing tool; I linked Google Docs for convenience, but Word is fine. Include what's currently on your mind, the questions you want to explore, the context or background, and any angles you're curious about. You have to include yourself in the prompt, or you'll get a very generic, uninteresting one. Order doesn't matter; AI will sort it out.

A very simple prompt gets a flat response, and it fluctuates: run it again and you'll get something different, because the model selects different tokens the second time through. A comprehensive prompt with context gives a better, more reliable response, since you've left fewer possibilities for variation. I also recommend not writing in the prompt window itself. It's cramped, and it's easy to trigger a response before you want one. Write elsewhere, think about it, and once you say "yes, that's what I want to know," paste it into Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini.
John Day~60:00–63:49
Then review the response. Agents can be extremely verbose, to the point of cognitive overload, and much popular writing has a small ratio of what's essential to what's written. You can control this in the prompt with "be brief" or "bulleted list." Another way around it is step four: visualize the response. Give this command: Create an HTML artifact and not an inline widget that visualizes and organizes the last response in this conversation. It turns a dense answer into something easier to digest. Billions of web pages have been created since the 1990s, and the agents trained on them know which fonts, colors and arrangements work, so they take the words of the response, not your prompt, and figure out how to represent them on a page.
Helaine Silverman & John Day63:49–65:39
Helaine asked what an inline widget is. John Day: It's a newer feature that displays a graphic within the conversation flow, and the problem is that you can't edit it. An HTML artifact is a full web page you can edit, and you can add your own prompt to the visualization. I put sample narrative prompts on the portal to show what worked for me, though you should write your own, about things you care about.
"You have to include yourself in the prompt. Otherwise, you're going to get a very generic, uninteresting prompt." John Day
The Visualize Command
"Create an HTML artifact and not an inline widget that visualizes and organizes the last response in this conversation." The portal includes a copy button for it.
Prompting Checklist
  • Write a free-form narrative first, outside the prompt window
  • Include what's on your mind, your questions, context and angles
  • Simple prompts give flat, variable answers; rich prompts give steadier ones
  • Ask for "brief" or "bulleted" to control length
  • Optionally ask for an HTML artifact to organize the reply
