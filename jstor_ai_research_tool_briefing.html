JSTOR's AI Research Tool
read alongside the tool it describes

A briefing on the design, guardrails, and classroom use of JSTOR's item-level AI research assistant — built so it can only speak from the document in front of it.

Presenters: Dana Howard · Kelsey Schaefer · Nicole Green Format: 60-min webinar + live Q&A Tool live since: July 18, 2025 Audience: Librarians & faculty

JSTOR's AI research tool sits on the item page of a journal article, book chapter, or report — not in general search — and answers questions using only the document a reader has open. 00:04:18Ithaka frames the tool around its nonprofit mission: keep content central to research rather than replacing critical engagement with it. It can summarize key points, recommend related topics and items, and answer free-text questions, with every answer backed by an inline citation pointing to the exact line in the source. 00:05:42Citations link directly to the passage in the text that the answer drew from.

Institutional admins can disable it for all their users from a single checkbox in the JSTOR admin portal.

In the demo

What using it looks like

  • The chat opens automatically by explaining how your search term connects to the document you clicked into.
  • Built-in prompts: Show abstract (or What is this text about? when there's no abstract), Show related content, and Recommended topics.
  • Free-text questions work as plain language, not formal queries — "which books are mentioned" works as well as a full question.
  • Every answer carries a numbered citation; hovering it previews the exact passage it came from.
  • Highlighting a passage (longer than a paragraph) and asking "what is this selection about?" scopes the answer to just that section.
  • Conversation history can be downloaded as a PDF or HTML transcript — useful as its own teaching artifact.

Design principles

Three guardrails, built in on purpose

01 — Academic integrity

Aid learning, don't replace it

Limited to summarizing, Q&A, and recommendations on one document. Requests for new essays or generated content are deliberately refused. 00:23:12The tool has manual stoppers so it cannot be used to generate new essays or content.

02 — Traceability

Show the receipts

Every response carries inline references to the exact passage used, connecting the answer back to the author's own words. 00:23:35Inline references point to the precise location in the source text behind each answer.

03 — Focus on the source

One document, nothing else

It never reaches outside the item being viewed — no open web, no rest of JSTOR — which cuts hallucination and says "not enough context" when it should. 00:24:12Restricting the tool to a single item is described as reducing hallucination and improving accuracy.

Under the hood

RAG, not a ChatGPT wrapper

The tool runs on retrieval-augmented generation: it sends the document's text plus the user's question to a large language model with instructions to answer using only that supplied content. 00:28:12RAG combines a language model with an external source, keeping the document content separate from the model's own training. Several models are used in rotation — including one from OpenAI and one from Anthropic via Amazon Bedrock — and JSTOR evaluates open-source alternatives where it can. 00:27:06The research tool uses multiple LLMs, including models from OpenAI and Anthropic, rather than a single provider.

A related but separate technology, vector embeddings, powers the new Semantic Results search tab and the tool's "recommended topics" — converting text into numerical representations to compare meaning, without any generative step involved. 00:33:14Vector embeddings are used for semantic search and recommendations, and are described as distinct from generative AI. Both the research tool and Semantic Results are also being trialed on the search-results page and inside the JSTOR Workspace, for a small beta group.

For the classroom

Two ways to put it in front of students

1 — Make the tool itself the lesson

Download a conversation transcript and have students annotate it alongside the original text (a social annotation tool like Hypothesis works well, or plain paper). The goal: discuss what kinds of questions get good answers, where the guardrails kick in, and why JSTOR avoided the word "summarize" in favor of "key points" — a deliberate signal that the output is a starting point, not a finished read. 00:44:01The tool avoids the word "summarize," framing its output as key points rather than a definitive condensation.

2 — Build it into a scaffolded assignment

Borrowing the "AI marble layer cake" model from Tufts' teaching center, a term paper gets broken into discrete steps — topic, background research, drafting, final product — with the research tool slotted into the early research stages, and students submitting their tool transcript as part of the deliverable. 00:45:49The layer-cake approach scaffolds AI use into specific steps of an assignment rather than leaving it unstructured. In-class, less AI-susceptible formative work fills in around it.

What people actually asked

Is my data or chat history being used to train anything?No. Only the prompt and the document text go to the model, solely to generate that answer; it isn't used for training and isn't kept past 30 days, per the providers' API policies. 00:50:53Data sent to the model is used only to produce the response and isn't retained beyond 30 days.
Why does it require a personal login?It's a deliberate, possibly temporary, choice to manage a sustainable rollout — and it piggybacks on the same account already used for JSTOR Workspace.
Does it use author content to train AI models?No — explicitly not, addressing the most common concern from rights-holders. 00:55:11JSTOR states plainly that content on the platform is not used to train large language models.
Does it work over a library proxy?Yes — proxy access works fine as long as the personal account is also logged in.
It's not showing up for my students — why?Either they're missing one of the two access requirements, or an institutional admin has switched it off.
