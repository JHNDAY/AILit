An Anatomy of a Prompt for:
The Crossing Lines

How one question about liberal arts, code, and AI turned into a lesson on reading AI's confidence — five drafts at a time.

The text below never changed its topic. What changed, draft by draft, was who it was for, what it was supposed to accomplish, how success would be measured, how much confidence the answer was allowed to claim, and finally, what form the answer should take. This is a record of that sequence.

Version 1 — Subject

✓ Subject Identity Purpose Audience Success Criterion Intellectual Frame
I have recently read several articles that the rapid development for AI will open up many new job opportunities for people with Liberal arts degrees. In a rather stunning change of roles it appears that due to AI the value of a liberal arts degree may be increasing whereas the value of a degree in programming maybe rapidly decreasing. Analyze and describe the causes for this increase/decrease of job opportunities in these traditionally disjoint education tracks. Include citations and links to those citations. Comment on how this very exercise illustrates the point.
✦ What's working

The phrase "a rather stunning change of roles" signals genuine curiosity, not a request for a flat encyclopedia entry. And "comment on how this very exercise illustrates the point" already builds a self-referential loop — a sophisticated move most prompt-writers never think to make.

→ What's still missing

No audience and no purpose are named. Claude has to guess whether this is for serious labor-market forecasting or a teaching moment — and that guess will shape tone, depth, and emphasis more than anything else in the prompt.

✦ What's working

→ What's still missing

The pedagogical priority is named but not yet operationalized. Without a more direct instruction, Claude will likely still treat the meta-comment as a short closing coda rather than the main event the prompt now claims it should be.

"Keep ... concise; spend more space on ..." explicitly redirects Claude's effort budget, and names three concrete teaching targets — reasoning, sourcing, blind spots — instead of leaving "the pedagogical point" vague.

→ What's still missing

The prompt asks Claude to describe AI's blind spots in general, but not yet to turn that lens on itself. Nothing here requires Claude to flag its own uncertainty in this specific analysis.

Version 4 — Intellectual Frame

I have recently read several articles that the rapid development for AI will open up many new job opportunities for people with Liberal arts degrees. In a rather stunning change of roles it appears that due to AI the value of a liberal arts degree may be increasing whereas the value of a degree in programming maybe rapidly decreasing. Comment on how this very exercise illustrates the point.

it is for the AILit SIG, where the pedagogical point about AI literacy itself matters more than the labor-market forecasting

Keep the labor-market analysis concise; spend more space on what this exercise itself teaches my SIG members about evaluating AI-generated reasoning, sourcing, and where AI's blind spots are.

Be explicit about which claims in your own analysis are well-supported versus speculative, and flag any places where you may be overstating certainty.
✦ What's working

This forces real calibration. "Well-supported versus speculative" turns the blind-spot lesson from something Claude describes in the abstract into something it has to perform and own, claim by claim, in this exact analysis.

→ What's still missing

The output is still a wall of prose. Members have no way to see the well-supported/speculative distinction at a glance — the calibration lives in sentences, not in a structure they can scan during a session.

✦ What's working

This converts the whole exercise into an artifact the room can look at — color-coded confidence, a source ledger, a chart that admits where it's illustrative. The calibration from V4 becomes a structure, not just a paragraph.

→ What's still missing

The confidence ratings are still Claude's own judgment calls, with no built-in mechanism for the room to contest or re-rate them directly inside the artifact — that re-rating has to happen in discussion, not on the page.

Key Lesson

Five small additions, and the topic never changed — yet the task Claude was solving changed completely. The first version asked for an answer; each version after named who the answer was for, what it should accomplish, how to measure success, how confident to sound, and finally what form it should take. None of these additions made the prompt longer in any meaningful way — each one simply removed a guess Claude would otherwise have to make on its own. The lesson for prompt-writing generally: a clear topic is necessary but not sufficient. Precision comes from naming the reader, the goal, the standard of evidence, and the deliverable — in that order, a prompt stops describing a subject and starts directing a piece of work.
